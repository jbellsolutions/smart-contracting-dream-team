# NZ email, text and privacy rules, in plain words

**Not legal advice.** This is a plain summary to help Jesse ask the right questions. Where we're not sure of a detail, it says so. Have your lawyer check how it applies to Smart Contracting before campaigns start.

## 1. The Unsolicited Electronic Messages Act 2007

The Act covers **commercial electronic messages**: emails, texts and similar messages that promote a business, its services or a business opportunity, with a New Zealand link. The Department of Internal Affairs enforces it, and the penalties can be significant.

For every commercial message, three things:

1. **Consent.** You must not send, **or cause to be sent**, a commercial message to someone who hasn't consented. Consent can be:
   - **Express:** they said yes (signed up, ticked a box, asked to hear from you).
   - **Inferred:** reasonably inferred from their conduct and an existing business or other relationship (for example, a past customer).
   - **Deemed:** their work email address is **conspicuously published** (say on their business website), there's no note next to it saying they don't want unsolicited messages, and your message is relevant to their business or role.
2. **Accurate sender details.** Say who authorised the message and how to contact them.
3. **A working unsubscribe.** Every message needs a way to opt out that actually works, and an unsubscribe request must be honoured **within 5 working days**. The Act also says the unsubscribe has to keep working for a period after the message is sent; check the exact period with your lawyer.

Some messages that only give factual information about something already agreed (for example, confirming an arrangement) aren't treated as commercial. If in doubt, treat a message as commercial and follow all three rules.

The Act also restricts using **address-harvesting software** and **harvested address lists** for unsolicited commercial messages.

### (a) Smart Contracting's cold emails to businesses

These go to building inspectors, agents, property managers and trades, at their work addresses. The likely basis is **deemed consent**:

- The address has to be **published** where people can see it, usually on their own website or a directory listing. If an address was guessed or built from a pattern (like firstname@business) rather than published, deemed consent may not apply.
- Check the page for a "no unsolicited emails" note. If there is one, don't email that address.
- The email has to be **relevant to their role**. Introducing a drainage service for their clients to someone in property is relevant. Keep it that way.
- Jesse's emails already include his name, business and phone (sender details) and his opt-out line: "If you don't see us working together, no worries at all. Just reply and let me know and I won't email again." That's a reply-to-unsubscribe, which counts if replies are actually acted on. The suppression list takes people off straight away, well inside 5 working days.
- **Ask your lawyer:** how the address-harvesting rules apply to the way the Revenue Partner finds and checks addresses on public websites.

### (b) Partners emailing their own customers

The partner sends the campaign, so the partner needs consent from everyone on their list:

- **Express consent** (they signed up to the partner's newsletter or updates) is the clearest.
- **Inferred consent** from being the partner's past client is less clear here, because the message promotes **another business** (Smart Contracting). Whether a past client relationship with an agent or inspector covers a message about a drainage company is genuinely unclear. Ask your lawyer.
- The campaign templates (`campaigns/`) say the email comes from the partner, carry the partner's details and end with an unsubscribe line in the partner's name. The partner must honour unsubscribes on their own list within 5 working days.
- **Smart Contracting's side:** the Act covers anyone who **causes** a message to be sent. Because Smart Contracting writes the copy and pays a fee on results, it may count as causing the partner's message to be sent. Sensible steps: only run campaigns with partners who confirm they're allowed to email their list, keep that confirmation in writing, and never take or use their list. **Ask your lawyer** how much responsibility this puts on Smart Contracting.
- **Texts** follow the same rules as emails.

### (c) Smart Contracting's own past customers

Past customers and people who asked for a quote probably give **inferred consent** for relevant messages from Smart Contracting itself, but how old the relationship is and how relevant the message is both matter. Every message still needs sender details and a working unsubscribe. Check with your lawyer before any email or text goes to past customers, and before Smart Contracting ever tells its customers about a partner.

## 2. The Privacy Act 2020

The Privacy Act covers any information about an identifiable person: names, phone numbers, addresses, and details of their job.

**What matters for referrals:**

- **Best:** the customer contacts Smart Contracting themselves and quotes the partner's code. Then we're collecting their details straight from them.
- **If a partner passes on a customer's name and number,** the partner needs the customer's OK first, and we should mention how we got their details when we first call.
- **Collect only what's needed:** name, contact, suburb and the job. Use it for that job and the partner fee, nothing else.
- **Keep it secure.** If personal information is lost or accessed by someone who shouldn't have it, and that's likely to cause serious harm, you must tell the Privacy Commissioner and the people affected as soon as practicable.
- **Tell partners only what they need.** A partner needs to know that a referral came in and that a fee is due. They don't need the customer's job details or the invoice amount. That's why remittance notes show the referral number and suburb only. Because the fee is 5% of the job value, a partner could work out roughly what the job cost; ask your lawyer whether the customer should be told that at the start.
- **People can ask** to see and correct what we hold about them.
- **Every business needs a privacy officer.** For Smart Contracting that's probably Jesse.
- **Where it's stored:** partner and referral records sit on the computer the Revenue Partner runs on. Ask your lawyer whether anything about where that computer is hosted needs to go in your privacy statement.
- **No customer list changes hands.** Partners keep their lists. We never receive, copy or use them.

## 3. Rules for particular partners

- **Real-estate agents** are licensed under the Real Estate Agents Act 2008 and have client-care rules about conflicts of interest and benefits they receive. We're not sure exactly how those rules apply to a referral fee from a drainage company. Ask the agent, and your lawyer, before agreeing a fee with an agent. The draft agreement makes the partner responsible for any disclosure their own rules require.
- **Building inspectors** may have their own expectations about independence when recommending a contractor. We don't know of a specific rule; ask.
- **Tax:** the fee is income for the partner, and GST may apply. Whether the 5% is on the amount before or after GST, and whether partners add GST, is still Jesse's decision: [GST basis: to confirm with Jesse], [partner adds GST to their fee: to confirm with Jesse].
